How to teach it (2 minutes)
  1. Every day, about 5 minutes. Short and often beats long. If they want more, do the next lesson. Stop while it's still fun.
  2. Read only the “You say” lines. The script is short on purpose. The child's job is the big letters.
  3. Hold the long sounds, clip the short ones. “mmm” and “sss” last about two seconds. “t” and “d” are quick, with no “tuh” or “duh”.
  4. Fix mistakes right away, kindly. Tap ✗ and the app shows you exactly what to say: my turn, together, your turn. Missed items come back at the end of the part.
  5. Praise the work. “You read that word!” beats “good job”.
  6. In the car: your child holds the phone or tablet in the back seat. The app tells you when to say a sound or a slow word. Say “yes” when it's right, “again” when it isn't, or “repeat”, “skip” or “stop”. If reading in the car makes your child queasy, stick to the “Say it fast” listening games.
  7. Firm before moving on. If fewer than 7 in 10 were right the first time, the app asks you to repeat the lesson. That's normal.
What is Direct Instruction?

Direct Instruction is a way of teaching developed by Siegfried “Zig” Engelmann and colleagues starting in the 1960s. Lessons are carefully sequenced and scripted, new things come one at a time with lots of review, and mistakes are corrected on the spot. In the 1970s it had the strongest results of any approach tested in Project Follow Through, one of the largest education studies ever run.

The story is told in season 2 of the Sold a Story podcast from APM Reports. Episode 5, “Why It Works”, explains the theory behind it.
